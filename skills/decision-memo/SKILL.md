# Decision memo

Turn an executive's notes into a memo that peers or a leadership team can act on. The executive owns the facts, recommendation, and final wording. Draft the ask without making the decision, implying approval, or sending the memo.

## Procedure

1. **Gather inputs.** Ask for the audience and who decides, the exact decision and deadline, why it matters now, relevant facts and constraints, viable options, the executive's preferred option (if any), key risks, and next steps with owners and dates. Ask targeted questions about missing or unclear inputs rather than asking for information already supplied.
2. **Check the evidence.** Separate supplied facts from estimates, assumptions, and proposals. Label each estimate or assumption where it matters. Never invent facts, figures, deadlines, or commitments. If a material gap remains, mark it explicitly and ask for confirmation before circulation.
3. **Compare the options.** Present two or three viable choices, including the status quo if it is a real choice. Compare them on the same decision-relevant criteria, such as cost, timing, and impact. Show what each option gains and gives up; if fewer than two viable options are known, ask what else is feasible rather than padding the list.
4. **Explain the recommendation.** State which option the executive recommends, why it beats the strongest alternative, and what trade-off it accepts. If the executive has not chosen a recommendation, ask for their judgment; label any suggestion as a proposal for their review. Pair the biggest risks with mitigations or contingency plans, and make next steps conditional on the decision.
5. **Draft for a quick read.** Put the decision and deadline first. Use plain language, short paragraphs, and bullets or a compact table. Keep the memo to one page. After the draft, list only the material questions the executive needs to resolve; omit the list if there are none.

## Output format

# Decision memo — [Topic]

**Decision needed by [date]:** State exactly what the recipients are being asked to approve or choose. Say what happens if the deadline slips, if known.

**Context** — Briefly explain why the decision is needed now and which facts or constraints narrow the choices.

**Options and trade-offs** — Two or three bullets or table rows. For each option, state the main benefit and cost or downside using comparable criteria.

**Recommendation** — Name the preferred option, explain why, and acknowledge the trade-off.

**Risks and mitigations** — Pair each material risk with how it will be handled. Note any risk that must be accepted or any mitigation that still needs confirmation.

**Next steps if approved** — List the actions, with an owner and date for each. Mark anything not confirmed.

**Open questions** — Include only material gaps that need executive input before circulation; omit if none.

## Example of good output

# Decision memo — Support weekend coverage

**Decision needed by May 15:** Approve a $12,000 weekend support pilot or keep weekday-only coverage. A later decision would miss the vendor's June start window.

**Context** — Support averaged 40 weekend tickets per week in April. Those tickets currently wait until Monday for a first response.

**Options and trade-offs**
- **Keep weekday-only coverage.** No added spend, but weekend tickets still wait until Monday.
- **Run a four-week vendor pilot.** Spend $12,000 to answer routine tickets on weekends. Complex tickets still wait for the weekday team.

**Recommendation** — Run the pilot. It tests whether weekend coverage reduces wait times without committing to an ongoing contract. The trade-off is paying for coverage that cannot resolve complex cases.

**Risks and mitigations** — The vendor may give incorrect answers. Support Operations will limit the vendor to approved replies and review a sample each week. The pilot ends after four weeks unless leadership approves an extension.

**Next steps if approved**
- **Procurement:** Sign the four-week agreement by May 22.
- **Support Operations:** Train the vendor by May 29 and report response times and reply accuracy by July 8.

This example uses invented figures and dates. Use only supported facts in the executive's actual memo.
